Security and data handling

Cerberus is a closed-source, local-only password manager. You cannot read the code, so this page explains in plain terms what the app does with your data, what you can check for yourself, and where its limits are. It is a description, not a promise that nothing can go wrong.

The short version

  • Your vault is encrypted with AES-256-GCM and stored only on your phone.
  • The app has no internet permission, so Android itself stops it from sending data off the device.
  • You have one master password. It is stretched with Argon2id, tuned on your own phone, so guessing it offline is slow.
  • A PIN or fingerprint is an optional shortcut, and the app asks for the master password again regularly.
  • Nobody, including me, can recover your vault if you lose your master password and your recovery.
  • The app has not been independently audited. The last section says where that stands.

How the vault is encrypted

The cipher. Vault contents are encrypted with AES-256-GCM using a random 256-bit vault key. A fresh random value is used for every encryption, and GCM detects tampering.

Your master password. It goes through Argon2id, which is deliberately slow and memory-hungry. When you set it up, Cerberus times the phone and tunes the settings, starting from 64 MiB of memory, 3 passes and 2 lanes and raising the memory on faster phones (to at most 256 MiB and never more than 10% of the phone's RAM), aiming for roughly a second per attempt. Cerberus rates your password as you choose it and requires a minimum length and strength. That rating is an estimate, not a guarantee, and no setting can rescue a weak password, so use a long one.

The vault key is never stored as-is. It is stored locked several times over, once for each way in: your master password, your PIN if you set one, your fingerprint if you set one, and your recovery phrase and image together. Each lock is derived only from its own factors, so a wrong one opens nothing, and your recovery phrase or image on their own open nothing.

Your PIN. The 6 to 8 digit PIN is mixed with a key held in the Android Keystore that cannot leave the phone. A copy of the vault files taken off your phone therefore cannot be attacked through the PIN. Five wrong PIN attempts destroy that Keystore key, which ends the PIN for good; you then use your master password.

Fingerprint. Only Android's strong biometrics are accepted, bound to a hardware-backed key. Cerberus never sees your fingerprint; it only receives a yes or no from Android.

When the master password is required

A PIN or fingerprint is convenient, but it is the weaker door, so Cerberus asks for the master password:

  • every time the app is started fresh after being closed or the phone restarts,
  • after the screen has been off longer than a limit you choose (4 hours by default),
  • at least once every 24 hours by default (you can choose 1, 4, 8 or 24 hours),
  • if the phone's clock is moved backwards or no longer agrees with the phone's own uptime clock, which stops an easy way of dodging the limits,
  • and for sensitive actions when you opened the app with a PIN or fingerprint: changing security settings, exporting, showing the sync password and opening hidden vaults. (Making a backup is allowed, but see the next sections: such a backup always needs your recovery phrase as well.)

None of these ever erase anything. Wrong master passwords on the lock screen are slowed down: five tries are free, then the wait starts at 30 seconds and doubles up to an hour. The wait slows a person holding your phone; it does not stop someone who copies the vault file and guesses offline, which is why the master password itself must be strong.

Recovery

A 20-word recovery phrase and a recovery image are the way back in if you forget your master password. You need both together. The image is a picture, either one you choose or one Cerberus draws, with a secret hidden in its pixels that only your phrase can locate. It only works as the exact PNG file you saved, so screenshots, crops, resizes and re-compression break it. Use the Test button in Settings after saving it. When you recover, Cerberus replaces the vault key and your master password and re-encrypts the vault. Anyone who holds both parts can use them, so keep them offline and apart from your phone and from each other.

Where your data lives and what the app can reach

  • Your vault is one file, vault.bin, in the app's private storage on the phone (/data/data/com.cerberus.app/files/). Other apps cannot read that folder. On a rooted phone it can be copied, which is why it is encrypted and why your master password matters.
  • Small settings, such as when you last entered your master password, are kept in the app's private preferences, and the keys behind the PIN and fingerprint live in the Android Keystore. No vault contents are stored in them. Android's own cloud backup of the app is turned off.
  • Photo attachments (for example the front and back of a licence) are shrunk, stripped of location data and encrypted with a separate key. They are not included in backups or sync.
  • The app requests biometric authentication and NFC (for the optional security-key mode). It has no internet, contacts, location, storage, camera or microphone permission.
  • Screenshots and screen recording are blocked unless you allow them in Settings. Copied passwords are flagged sensitive and cleared from the clipboard after the delay you choose.

Backups and sync

  • Encrypted backups (.cerb files) use a password you choose (10 characters or more), Argon2id and AES-256-GCM. An optional switch makes a backup also require your recovery phrase to open it, which means a stolen backup file is useless without the phrase. If you opened the app with a PIN or fingerprint rather than the master password, the app still lets you make a backup, but then always requires the phrase as well. Backups never contain attached pictures. Anyone who holds a backup file can try to guess its password offline, so choose a long one.
  • Personal sync (Pro) writes one encrypted file per device into a folder you choose and merges the files from your other devices. The app itself makes no network connection. Whatever keeps that folder in step, such as Syncthing or Nextcloud, is a separate app and is the one that touches the network.

Autofill, passkeys and security-key mode

  • Autofill only offers your data after you unlock Cerberus. It matches logins by the website or app saved on the item. Android decides where suggestions can appear, so a malicious app can still read what you let it fill; fill only into apps you trust.
  • Passkeys (Android 14 or newer) are stored as items in your encrypted vault. Cerberus checks the requesting app by its signing certificate. Passkey support is new, so keep another way into important accounts.
  • Security-key mode (Pro, beta) lets your phone act as a FIDO2 key over NFC, using only passkeys from your main vault; hidden vaults never act as a key. It answers only while you have armed it on screen with the vault unlocked, for 60 seconds and a single use. It has not been tested against every reader.

Hidden vaults (Pro)

Pro adds extra vaults, each opened by its own PIN. They protect against someone picking up your phone and using the app. They do not hide that Cerberus is installed, and a skilled examiner with full access to an unlocked or rooted phone may still find signs of it. Some options destroy data the moment a code is entered, and that cannot be undone. Test with throwaway data first.

What Cerberus cannot protect against

  • A phone that is rooted or infected with malware.
  • Someone watching you type your master password, or a keylogger on the phone.
  • A weak or reused master password.
  • A recovery phrase or image kept where other people can find them.
  • Being forced to unlock the app.
  • While the vault is unlocked, your data sits in the phone's memory in ordinary form, as it does in every app that shows you your data. Locking the app clears it as far as Android allows, but no app can guarantee that memory is wiped.

What you can check yourself

You do not need the source code to verify the biggest claims.

  1. No internet access. In Android Settings, open Apps, then Cerberus, then Permissions. The internet permission is not listed because the app never requested it. Without it Android blocks the app's network access. A permission-scanning app such as Exodus Privacy will show the same.
  2. Where the vault is. The file location above is where to look. With a rooted phone or a debugging connection you can see that the vault is one opaque file with no readable contents.
  3. Is this the genuine build? Builds installed outside the Play Store are signed with a key whose SHA-256 fingerprint is:
    E3:4F:41:A9:D6:16:6B:8A:74:F6:52:CE:72:BF:6E:96:DB:FC:00:CB:0A:63:B4:8F:B5:0F:94:70:A8:75:5E:F3
    You can compare it using apksigner verify --print-certs on the APK. Play Store builds are signed by Google's Play App Signing instead, so they will show a different fingerprint.
  4. Your own backup test. Make an encrypted backup, restore it on a spare phone, and confirm it works before you rely on it.

Where things stand

I want to be straight about the gaps:

  • The code is closed and has not had an independent security audit. Treat the app as unaudited.
  • It is built and maintained by one person.
  • A separate beta build is offered for download before the Play Store release. It can have bugs and lose data; see Downloads.
  • Passkeys, security-key mode and the newest autofill features are young and have not been proven against every website and device.

What I do commit to: keeping this page accurate whenever the app changes, publishing release notes that say what changed in security-relevant code, and acting on reports quickly. An independent audit is something I want to pay for when I can afford it, and I will publish the summary if it happens, but there is no date and no commitment yet.
